A little clarity
goes a long way.

Plain-language explanations of the terms, powers and institutions you encounter across these laws. These explain their use here; they are not replacement statutory definitions.

45 terms

Bailable

Bail as a right

An offense classification under which bail should be granted as a matter of right. It is separate from whether police may arrest without a warrant; both speech offenses in the Cyber Protection Act are bailable.

See Bailable in the Cyber Protection Act guide

BTRC

Bangladesh Telecommunication Regulatory Commission

The regulator established under the Bangladesh Telecommunication Act. Our analysis examines its licensing and information-access powers, expanded remit and dependence on executive appointments and direction.

See BTRC in the Bangladesh Telecommunication Act guide

Cognizable

Arrest without a warrant

An offense classification allowing police to arrest without a warrant and open an investigation without prior judicial approval. It does not mean the accused is guilty. The Cyber Protection Act also requires prompt magistrate scrutiny of complaints under its two speech offenses.

See Cognizable in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Competition Commission

Bangladesh Competition Commission

The competition regulator established under the Competition Act. Its members are not required to have digital-market expertise.

See Competition Commission in the Competition Act guide

Consent

Permission to process data

One legal basis for collecting and using personal data. The Personal Data Protection Act also allows other grounds, including contractual necessity and protection of life or health, so processing without consent is not automatically unlawful.

See Consent in the Personal Data Protection Act guide

Consideration

What is exchanged for a service

The value given in an exchange. Consumer law’s traditional payment-based approach does not adequately recognize the personal data and attention users provide for many digital services.

See Consideration in the Consumer Rights Protection Act guide

Criminal defamation

Reputational harm addressed through criminal law

The Penal Code addresses defamation through criminal proceedings and penalties, with specified exceptions.

See Criminal defamation in the Penal Code guide

CSAM

Child sexual abuse material

Material in forms including images, video, audio, text or digital media that depicts, describes, promotes, facilitates or involves sexual exploitation, abuse, grooming or sexually explicit depictions of a child, whether real or simulated. This is the terminology used in this edition.

See CSAM in the Pornography Control Act guide

Dark patterns

Design that undermines informed choice

Manipulative interfaces such as confusing consent flows, deceptive button placement, disguised advertising and obstructive cancellation.

See Dark patterns in the Consumer Rights Protection Act guide

Data breach

Personal information is exposed

A security incident involving personal data that may expose people to identity theft, fraud, reputational harm or other risks. The reporting duty turns on the likelihood of significant damage, rather than requiring every breach to be reported.

See Data breach in the Personal Data Protection Act guide

Data localization

Keeping data within national systems

Requirements to store data domestically, including domestic copies. We distinguish these from the narrower notification-based transfer framework, while identifying powers that could indirectly require local storage.

See Data localization in the overlap map

Data minimization

Limit how much data is collected or accessed

Keeping data collection and access proportionate to the purpose, rather than gathering unrelated information.

See Data minimization in the Personal Data Protection Act guide

Data subject

The person the data is about

The individual whose personal data is collected or processed and whose rights are at stake. The data-protection framework includes rights to correction, withdrawal of consent, objection and deletion.

See Data subject in the Personal Data Protection Act guide

DNCRP

Directorate of National Consumers’ Right Protection

The consumer-protection body established under the 2009 Act. DG-DNCRP means its Director General.

See DNCRP in the Consumer Rights Protection Act guide

Due process

Fair procedures when powers are exercised

Safeguards such as clear legal thresholds, notice, an opportunity to challenge decisions, independent review and fair handling of evidence. We use these to assess whether enforcement protects people against arbitrary decisions.

See Due process in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Erasure

Deletion of personal data

A data-protection right to deletion on grounds such as consent withdrawal or unlawful processing. The framework also requires approved deletions to spread across connected systems. Its use needs to be balanced against expression and public-interest information.

See Erasure in the Personal Data Protection Act guide

Ex post review

Review after an action has occurred

Scrutiny after a measure, such as interception or content removal, has already happened. It can assess the action afterwards but does not provide the same protection as independent authorization beforehand.

See Ex post review in the Bangladesh Telecommunication Act guide

Extraterritoriality

A law’s reach beyond national borders

Application of a domestic law to people, businesses or conduct outside the country. Several of the laws reviewed here either claim that reach without clear thresholds or leave it unclear for offshore platforms.

See Extraterritoriality in the Consumer Rights Protection Act guide

Function creep

Data used beyond its original purpose

The expansion of a system or dataset into uses beyond the context for which information was originally collected. Connecting identity records across public services can increase this risk without enforceable limits on reuse.

See Function creep in the National Data Management Act guide

GDPR

General Data Protection Regulation

The European Union’s personal-data-protection regulation. It is a comparative reference for rights, oversight and enforcement in this edition; it is not Bangladesh’s data-protection law.

See GDPR in the Personal Data Protection Act guide

ICCPR

International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

A United Nations human-rights treaty and one of the benchmarks used to assess these laws, alongside Bangladesh’s Constitution and other rights instruments.

See ICCPR in how we assessed the laws

Intermediary

A service that carries or hosts others’ content

A provider that receives, transmits, stores, processes or hosts electronic records or communications for others. An intermediary is not necessarily the creator or publisher of the content it carries.

See Intermediary in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Interoperability

Systems working and exchanging data together

The ability of systems to connect and exchange information. In public services it can support coordination; in platform markets, restrictions on it can limit switching and competition. Interoperability need not require centralized storage.

See Interoperability in the Competition Act guide

Judicial authorization

Permission from a court before action

Approval by a court or judicial authority before an intrusive measure takes place. It differs from reviewing the action afterwards.

See Judicial authorization in the Cyber Protection Act guide

LEA

Law enforcement agency

Here, this includes Bangladesh Police as well as intelligence agencies. It describes a group of institutions, not a single regulator.

See LEA in the security-first thread

NCPA

National Cyber Protection Agency

The agency established under the Cyber Protection Act. Its subordination to the Information and Communication Technology Division and reliance on future rules raise concerns about independent protection of citizens’ interests.

See NCPA in the Cyber Protection Act guide

NCPC

National Cyber Protection Council

The council established under the Cyber Protection Act. Its state-dominated composition and operation under the prime minister’s direction are central to our concerns about cyber-governance independence.

See NCPC in the Cyber Protection Act guide

NDMA

National Data Management Authority

The authority created by the National Data Management Act. It oversees data governance and processing in both public and private sectors. The acronym refers here to the authority, not the Act itself.

See NDMA in the National Data Management Act guide

NDMPB

National Data Management Policy Board

The policy board established under the National Data Management Act. Its composition includes seventeen ministers, officials and government appointees, and seven government-nominated experts.

See NDMPB in the National Data Management Act guide

Network effects

A service gains value as participation grows

A platform can become more valuable as more users join, including across groups such as consumers and advertisers. This can strengthen market power even where users pay no subscription fee.

See Network effects in the Competition Act guide

Non-bailable

Bail depends on judicial discretion

Bail is not granted as a matter of right; a court decides whether to grant it. It does not mean bail can never be granted. The Pornography Control Act classifies its offenses this way.

See Non-bailable in the Pornography Control Act guide

Non-cognizable

A warrant is required for arrest

An offense classification requiring a warrant for arrest. It is distinct from bailability: the identity-based hatred offense in the Cyber Protection Act is both non-cognizable and bailable.

See Non-cognizable in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Non-consensual intimate imagery

Intimate material used without consent

A broader term than “revenge porn”: the abuse need not be motivated by revenge, and it can include threats to share, non-consensual recording and synthetic imagery.

See Non-consensual intimate imagery in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Notice-and-action

A process for reporting and responding to unlawful content

A framework for reporting content, assessing a notice and deciding an appropriate response, rather than treating every report as a demand for automatic removal.

See Notice-and-action in the Cyber Protection Act guide

NRDEX

National Responsible Data Exchange

The national exchange established under the National Data Management Act to connect databases. Mandatory integration of foundational identity records makes it central to both public-service coordination and concerns about concentrated data power.

See NRDEX in the National Data Management Act guide

NTMC

National Telecommunication Monitoring Centre

The national intelligence, surveillance and interception agency under the Ministry of Home Affairs. It is the principal monitoring body in the centralized interception framework.

See NTMC in the Bangladesh Telecommunication Act guide

OTT

Over-the-top service

An application or content service delivered to users over telecommunications networks or the internet without requiring the provider to own dedicated network infrastructure.

See OTT in the Bangladesh Telecommunication Act guide

Proportionality

A response matched to the harm and purpose

The requirement to match restrictions, powers and penalties to their legitimate purpose and the seriousness of harm.

See Proportionality in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Purpose limitation

Keep data use tied to its purpose

Limiting use of information to the purpose for which it was collected, rather than allowing unrelated reuse across connected systems.

See Purpose limitation in the National Data Management Act guide

Safe harbor

Conditional protection from liability for others’ content

Protection for intermediaries from liability for third-party content, subject to relevant conditions. The Cyber Protection Act removed the protections that existed under earlier laws.

See Safe harbor in the Cyber Protection Act guide

Self-preferencing

A platform favors its own services

Using a platform’s position to give affiliated products or services an advantage, for example through search or marketplace rankings.

See Self-preferencing in the Competition Act guide

Soft law

Policies and guidance rather than a binding statute

Instruments such as strategies, guidelines and voluntary codes. They offer flexibility but may lack binding force and consistent enforcement.

TFSV

Technology-facilitated sexual violence

Sexual violence, coercion or exploitation enabled, amplified or carried out through digital technology. This includes non-consensual or manipulated sexual material, sextortion, cyberstalking, online sexual harassment and sharing explicit content without consent.

See TFSV in the Pornography Control Act guide

Tying and bundling

Linking access to other services or conditions

Practices that connect services or require acceptance of additional obligations. We raise concerns where these arrangements force extensive data sharing, restrict switching or block competitors; we do not treat every bundle as unlawful.

See Tying and bundling in the Competition Act guide

Walled gardens

An ecosystem that makes leaving or connecting difficult

Platform arrangements that restrict interoperability, concentrate data or create switching barriers. These can limit consumer choice and competitors’ access to digital markets.

See Walled gardens in the Competition Act guide
