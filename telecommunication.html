Bangladesh Telecommunication Act

Telecom, internet services and interception.

How it affects you.

Your communications can potentially be intercepted or surveilled.

For state security, public order or serious crime, the Ministry of Home Affairs or an authority it names can approve monitoring of your calls, messages and data for a set period, and service providers must assist.§97A

What it protects

  • In most cases, interception needs prior approval from a court or an authority the government sets up, and must be necessary, proportionate, time-limited, a last resort and fully logged.§97A
  • An independent review council periodically checks past interceptions for legality, necessity and accountability.§97A(7)

Where it falls short

  • After-the-fact review is different from independent permission before monitoring starts.
  • Broad grounds and emergency exceptions can weaken oversight, putting conversations, relationships and lawful activities at risk of surveillance and interception.

You run an internet-based service.

The 2026 amendments appear to extend the law to online intermediaries and over-the-top services, and the law requires a license to provide telecommunication services.§§2, 35

Where it falls short

  • The expanded definitions leave it unclear how the licensing requirement applies to digital platforms.
  • A platform could face licensing burdens and severe penalties designed for infrastructure and legacy network operators.
  • This is an uncertainty in the framework, not a settled claim that every website or app needs a telecom license.

The regulator asks for your data.

The telecom regulator can compel documents and information from service providers and others, and can publicly disclose what it obtains in investigations.§§84, 85

What it protects

  • A hearing is allowed before confidential information is disclosed.§85

Where it falls short

  • It can demand any information it reasonably believes it needs, limited only by what a civil court could not make you produce.
  • An undefined “public interest” lowers the bar for disclosing personal data and trade secrets.

The central interception platform

§97A

A central interception platform sits under the Ministry of Home Affairs and is open to multiple agencies; the NTMC remains the principal monitoring body.

Analysis

Where the framework falls short.

Amended in 2026, the law stretches telecom standards over the internet and builds a central surveillance system.

Order reversed

Approval inside government, review afterwards

§97A

The enacted law dropped prior authorization by an independent council. Approval can now come from an authority the government sets up, in an emergency that authority can approve interception on its own, and the independent council only reviews it afterwards.

Too wide

What the regulator can demand and publish

§§84, 85

The regulator can require information and disclose information obtained during investigations. Despite the hearing it allows first, broad discretion over “public interest” and weak safeguards create risks for personal and business information.

Who decides

Who controls the telecom regulator

§§9, 12

The government appoints the regulator’s commissioners. The law’s preamble describes an independent commission, but no provision requires it to act free of government influence.

Recommendation

Revert to pre-2026.

Separate telecom, online safety and surveillance

The law should be reverted to its pre-2026 form for telecom, with its internet reach moved to a new Online Safety Act and its surveillance powers to a new investigatory-powers law.
