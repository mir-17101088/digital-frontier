How it affects you.

Your communications fall within an interception operation.

What the law says

A central interception platform under the Ministry of Home Affairs is open to multiple law-enforcement and intelligence agencies, and service providers must give technical assistance.§97A

What it protects

  • Interception is subject to approval requirements and procedural safeguards against misuse.§97A
  • A review council examines interception after it takes place.§97A

Where it falls short

  • After-the-fact review is different from independent permission before monitoring starts.
  • Broad grounds and emergency exceptions can weaken oversight, putting conversations, relationships and lawful activities at risk.

You run an internet-based service.

What the law says

The 2026 amendments extend the Act to online intermediaries and over-the-top services, and the Act requires a license to provide telecommunication services.§§2, 35

Where it falls short

  • The expanded definitions leave it unclear how the licensing requirement applies to digital platforms.
  • A platform could face licensing burdens and severe penalties designed for network operators.
  • This is an uncertainty in the framework, not a settled claim that every website or app needs a telecom license.

The regulator asks for your data.

What the law says

The telecom regulator can compel documents and information from service providers and others, and can publicly disclose what it obtains in investigations.§§84, 85

What it protects

  • A hearing is allowed before confidential information is disclosed.§85

Where it falls short

  • It may demand “such other information” as it considers necessary, with no stated limits.
  • An undefined “public interest” lowers the bar for disclosing personal data and trade secrets.

A telecom law that now reaches online platforms

§§2, 3, 30

New definitions pull social media, over-the-top services and other internet platforms under telecom regulation.

“False” messages and national-unity offenses

§§66, 66A

Sending “false or fraudulent” messages or content carries up to five years and fines up to BDT 490,000,000. A broad range of conduct affecting national security, public order or economic interests carries up to five years and fines up to BDT 990,000,000.

The central interception platform

§97A

A central interception platform sits under the Ministry of Home Affairs and is open to multiple agencies; the NTMC remains the principal monitoring body.

Analysis

Where the framework falls short.

Amended in 2026, the Act stretches telecom rules over the internet and builds a central surveillance system. It is rated high-risk in all three dimensions.

Order reversed

Interception first, review afterwards

§97A

The enacted law downgraded prior authorization by an independent council to after-the-fact review, so surveillance can start first.

Too wide

What the regulator can demand and publish

§§84, 85

The regulator can require information and disclose information obtained during investigations. Despite the hearing it allows first, broad discretion over “public interest” and weak safeguards create risks for personal and business information.

Who decides

Who controls the telecom regulator

§§9, 12

The government appoints and removes the regulator’s commissioners, and the Act contains no explicit guarantee of its independence.

Recommendation

Revert to pre-2026.

Separate telecom, online safety and surveillance

We recommend reverting the Act to its pre-2026 form for telecom, moving its internet reach to a new Online Safety Act, and its surveillance powers to a new investigatory-powers law.
