The colonial governance logic.

Several of today’s digital laws borrow language, structures and enforcement philosophies from colonial-era statutes, keeping a punitive, executive-centered approach.

One code, three eras.

How the speech offenses in the Penal Code (PC) were written, added to and carried forward.

  1. Colonial era

    1. 1860

      The Penal Code (PC)

      Most of its speech offenses were first written in the nineteenth century, as remnants of a colonial past.

    2. 1898

      Indian Penal Code Amendment Act

      Later amendments, such as this one, added more of the speech offenses.

  2. Pakistan era

    1. 1950

      Pakistan Penal Code (Amendment) Act

      Another amendment carried further speech provisions into the Code.

    2. 1962movement

      Inducing students into politics

      §153B

      Introduced while students in East Pakistan were politically active, especially during the 1962 Education Movement.

    3. 1965

      Offering prizes to promote sales

      §294B

      Rewards, discounts and promotional offers became a crime.

  3. Bangladesh

    1. 1991

      Offenses against the state and public order

      §505A

      The one exception, added after the return to parliamentary democracy.

    2. Today

      Still in force, now online

      Their broad wording reaches social media posts, blogs and other digital communications.

Built to control, kept in force.

Then

Written for control.

Offenses that may once have seemed reasonable for keeping a subjugated population under control.

Still in force

Now

Kept in a constitutional democracy.

They now stand in direct tension with the Constitution’s promise of “the rule of law, fundamental human rights and freedom.”

What the old code still carries.

Dissent treated as crime

§§124A, 153A, 295A, 499

Offenses on sedition, public order, religion, defamation and student politics are overbroad and vaguely drafted, with low thresholds and disproportionate penalties.

Morality policed

§509

Insulting a woman’s “modesty” rests on a colonial-era idea of morality and a paternalistic view of who needs protecting.

Rules for an older economy

§294B

A ban written for a physical, local economy could now reach discount codes, cashback and loyalty programs.

What still protects you

  • Criticizing government measures or actions is not sedition, so long as it does not stir up hatred, contempt or disaffection.§124A
  • Students themselves are not barred from political activity or discourse.§153B
  • Criminal defamation carries ten exceptions, including truth for the public good and comment on public servants.§499

The pattern repeats in newer laws.

One kind of speech, three laws.

The old speech offenses also duplicate newer laws.

  1. 1860

    Penal Code (PC)§§124A, 153A, 295A, 499, 505A

    Keeps colonial-era offenses on sedition, enmity between groups, religious feelings and public order, plus criminal defamation.

    • Up to life imprisonment The most severe listed speech offenses
    • Up to 2 years and/or a fine Criminal defamation
  2. 2001

    Telecommunication Act (BTA)§§66, 66A

    Penalizes “false or fraudulent” messages and a broad range of online conduct.

    • Up to 5 years, a fine up to BDT 490,000,000, or both “False or fraudulent” messages
    • Up to 5 years, a fine up to BDT 990,000,000, or both A broad range of online conduct
  3. 2026

    Cyber Protection Act (CPA)§26

    Criminalizes posting religious, communal or ethnic hate speech that causes violence or anxiety. Arrest needs a warrant.

    • Up to 2 years, a fine up to BDT 1,000,000, or both

In England, sedition began as a common-law offense to suppress dissent against the monarchy.

Referenced to show different approaches, not as models for adoption.

New Zealand and Singapore

Crimes (Repeal of Seditious Offences) Amendment Act 2007 · Sedition (Repeal) Act 2021

Counter-example · India and Australia

Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 · National Security Legislation Amendment Act 2010

Kept the substance of sedition-type offenses under new names. These are poor examples to follow.

Bangladesh is not alone. Similar patterns of colonial-era statutes, executive overreach and weak institutional safeguards are evident in many parts of the world.
