Markets, platforms and dominant businesses.

How it affects you.

A platform steers you toward its own products or makes switching difficult.

What the law says

The Act prohibits anti-competitive agreements and abuse of a dominant position.§§15, 16

What it protects

  • Its abuse-of-dominance terms are, in principle, broad enough to reach self-preferencing, tying and bundling.§16(2)

Where it falls short

  • What you see and which services you can use may be shaped by platform power, not simply price.
  • The law lacks clear digital-market standards for assessing these practices.
  • A free service is not automatically anti-competitive; its effects on competition and consumers matter.

You use a platform without paying a subscription.

What the law says

The definitions of “relevant market” and “service” are built around traditional commercial exchanges, price, substitutability and geography.§§2(s), 2(t)

Where it falls short

  • Your attention, data and connections can still generate value and strengthen a platform’s position.
  • Focusing too narrowly on price may miss that power, while treating every low-price service as predatory could wrongly discourage competition.

An app store decides how you pay.

What the law says

Forcing apps onto a store’s own payment system is a form of tying, which the Act’s abuse-of-dominance terms may reach in principle.§16(2)

What it protects

  • The Competition Commission can enquire into conduct abroad that harms a market here.§22

Where it falls short

  • The Act does not expressly recognize app-store practices such as compulsory payment gateways and anti-steering limits.
  • Commission members need not have digital-market expertise.

  • Anti-competitive agreements§15

  • By price, substitutability and geography§2(s)

What it can reach
  • Conduct abroad that harms a market here§22
  • In principle: data restrictions, self-preferencing, interoperability limits, tying§16(2)
Analysis

Where the framework falls short.

The Competition Act was designed around traditional markets. Its definitions and enforcement framework do not yet capture how data, attention, network effects and platform restrictions shape competition.

Undefined

Whether the law reaches offshore platforms

§§3, 22

The Commission may enquire into overseas conduct that adversely affects the relevant market. However, explicit cross-border application and enforcement procedures remain unclear, creating uncertainty for offshore digital platforms.

Undefined

Definitions built for priced markets

§§2(s), 2(t)

“Relevant market” is defined by price and geography, which misses cross-border, multi-sided digital platforms.

“Service” gives little guidance on zero-price models, where users pay with data and attention rather than money.

What it cannot see: network effects, data concentration, attention markets, zero-price services, ecosystem lock-in and cross-market leveraging.

Too wide

Abuse of dominance in digital markets

§§15, 16

Some abuse-of-dominance provisions may cover restrictive data practices, self-preferencing and limits on interoperability in principle. Without clear digital-market standards, low- or zero-price strategies can be misread as predatory, while genuine abuse escapes scrutiny.

Who decides

Whether the regulator has digital expertise

§7

The qualification framework does not ensure that Competition Commission members have specialized digital-market expertise in algorithms, platform economics and data-driven business models.

Recommendation

Modernize.

A platform-aware competition framework

We recommend substantially modernizing the Act into a platform-aware competition framework, with digital-specific standards, explicit reach to offshore platforms and specialist expertise in the Commission.
