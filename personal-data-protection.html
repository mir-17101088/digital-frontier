Your everyday life

How it affects you.

Start with the situation, then distinguish the legal rule from our assessment of its consequences.

An organisation collects or keeps your information.

The law

Consent is one basis for processing your data, but sections 5 and 7 also permit processing on grounds including contractual necessity, legal duties and protection of life or health. Sections 12–14 provide for correction, consent withdrawal, objections and deletion.

Our analysis · What this means for you

You gain ways to challenge how your information is used, but refusing consent does not prohibit every use. Broad alternative grounds and weak operational guidance can make those rights harder to exercise. Deletion also needs safeguards where personal data appears in journalism or other public-interest content.

Information about you is exposed in a breach.

The law

Section 20 requires reporting to the National Data Management Authority where a breach is likely to cause significant damage to the people affected. A breach alone does not automatically trigger this reporting duty.

Our analysis · What this means for you

The organisation makes the initial judgment about likely harm. An unclear “significant” threshold creates room for underreporting, even when a smaller breach could expose people to fraud, identity theft or reputational harm and limit their ability to take protective measures.

A public authority processes your data without consent.

The law

Section 24 permits exemptions on grounds including national security, public order and crime investigation. It also preserves other data-protection obligations, limits disproportionate or unnecessary exemptions, and allows the authority to review misuse.

Our analysis · What this means for you

These are not blanket exemptions from every duty. Our concern is that broad grounds, weak independent oversight and unclear procedures may allow exceptional access to become routine, leaving you with less practical control over state use of your data.

Children’s consent · Section 9

Section 9 permits parental or guardian consent for children and people incapable of giving consent. It requires consideration of their rights and interests and prohibits behavioural tracking, profiling and targeted advertising directed at them.

When a breach must be reported · Section 20

Section 20 requires reporting a personal-data breach to the National Data Management Authority when it is likely to cause significant damage to affected individuals. A breach alone does not automatically trigger this duty.

State exemptions · Section 24

Section 24 permits exemptions on grounds including national security, public order and crime investigation. It also preserves other data-protection obligations, limits disproportionate or unnecessary exemptions, and allows the authority to review misuse. These are not blanket exemptions from every duty. Our concern is that broad grounds, weak independent oversight and unclear procedures may allow exceptional access to become routine, leaving you with less practical control over state use of your data.

Erasure and freedom of expression

The 'delete-everywhere' erasure right (ss.12,13,14) risks becoming a content-removal/censorship tool — a privacy right that can harm free expression

Public-sector accountability

The 2026 framework omits the earlier provisions expressly allowing administrative sanctions against government bodies and their personnel. Combined with broad exemptions, this risks an accountability gap; it does not mean every form of public-sector accountability has been abolished.
