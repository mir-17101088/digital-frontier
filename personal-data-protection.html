Personal data, consent and accountability.

How it affects you.

An organization collects or keeps your information.

What the law says

Consent is one basis for using your data, but the Act also allows use for reasons such as a contract, a legal duty or protecting life or health.§§5, 7

What it protects

  • You can correct, update or delete your data, withdraw consent, and object to processing.§§12–14
  • Approved changes and deletions must carry across connected systems.§14

Where it falls short

  • Refusing consent does not prohibit every use.
  • Broad alternative grounds and weak operational guidance can make these rights harder to exercise.
  • Deletion needs safeguards where personal data appears in journalism or other public-interest content.

Information about you is exposed in a breach.

What the law says

A breach alone does not trigger the reporting duty; the organization judges whether significant damage is likely.§20

What it protects

  • A breach likely to cause significant damage must be reported to the data authority.§20

Where it falls short

  • An unclear “significant” threshold leaves room for underreporting.
  • Smaller breaches can still expose people to fraud, identity theft or reputational harm, and limit their chance to protect themselves.

A public authority processes your data without consent.

What the law says

The Act exempts state authorities from consent on grounds including national security, public order and crime investigation.§24

What it protects

  • Where an exemption applies, the other data-protection duties still do.§24(2)
  • Exemptions are restricted where they hit rights disproportionately or lack legal authority.§24(4)
  • The data authority may review misuse of an exemption.§24(5)

Where it falls short

  • Broad grounds, weak independent oversight and unclear procedures may let exceptional access become routine.
  • That leaves you less practical control over state use of your data.

Your child uses an app.

What the law says

Processing a child’s data needs a parent’s or guardian’s consent and must take the child’s rights and interests into account.§9

What it protects

  • Behavioral tracking, profiling and targeted ads directed at children are prohibited.§9

Where it falls short

  • The Act does not say how parental consent is given or verified, or how age is assessed.
  • Parental consent can stay valid until 18, with no step for the young adult’s own consent.

Children’s data

§9

Processing children’s data requires parental or guardian consent and regard for the child’s rights and interests; behavioral tracking, profiling and targeted advertising aimed at children are prohibited.

The breach-reporting duty

§20

A breach must be reported to the data authority only where it is likely to cause significant damage to the people affected.

Analysis

Where the framework falls short.

Bangladesh’s first cross-sectoral data-protection law brings several positive developments, but broad powers are often paired with undefined legal standards and heavy reliance on future regulations.

Too wide

State exemptions

§24

State authorities can invoke “national security,” “public interest” and “public order” to process personal data without the same compliance duties, which risks normalizing broad state access.

National securityPublic interestPublic order

Too wide

Erasure and freedom of expression

§§12–14

The system-wide erasure right risks being used to remove content, affecting journalism, historical records and public-interest information.

Undefined

Consent without a way to verify it

§9

Verification, age assessment and the handover of consent at 18 are left unaddressed, which makes the protection hard to operate.

Undefined

A reporting threshold left undefined

§20

“Significant” is undefined and judged by the organization that suffered the breach, which invites underreporting.

Deferred

A grace period without a roadmap

§1(3)

The grace period comes with no stated rationale or roadmap, and it defers the data-officer duty that would help organizations prepare.

Who decides

Public-sector accountability

The 2026 framework omits the earlier provisions expressly allowing administrative sanctions against government bodies and their personnel. Combined with broad exemptions, this risks an accountability gap; it does not mean every form of public-sector accountability has been abolished.
