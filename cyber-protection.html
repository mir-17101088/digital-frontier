How it affects you.

A complaint is made about something you posted.

What the law says

The Act covers specified sexual harms, blackmail and identity-based hatred. Police may arrest for the first without a warrant; for the second they need one. Both are bailable as a matter of right.§§25, 26

What it protects

  • A magistrate reviews the evidence within 24 hours and can dismiss the complaint and order release.§31(3)–(4)
  • Only a person directly aggrieved, a representative or a law-enforcement officer may file a case.§40

Where it falls short

  • Posting online is not, by itself, an offense. However, the distinction matters before a case reaches trial. Vague offense definitions and inconsistent observance of arrest safeguards can expose people to detention and legal costs even when a complaint lacks merit.

Your content is taken down.

What the law says

Authorities can remove or block content first; a tribunal then has three days to approve that decision.§8

What it protects

  • If a tribunal has not approved a removal in time, the content must be restored.§8(4)

Where it falls short

  • Your content can disappear before a tribunal reviews the decision.
  • The framework does not clearly explain how you are notified, how you can challenge removal, or how restoration is enforced.
  • There is no avenue for users to report harmful content under this procedure.

An investigation reaches your phone or computer.

What the law says

Investigators can search, seize digital evidence and devices, and arrest, including without a warrant in the circumstances the Act covers.§§33–35

Where it falls short

  • Access to a device can expose personal or business information unrelated to the alleged offense.
  • There are no sufficiently clear rules for limiting access, securing the data, and returning or destroying what was seized.

Your platform hosts someone else’s post.

What the law says

A platform can be held liable indirectly, for aiding an offense. Its owners, managers and staff are then liable unless they show they did not know or made reasonable efforts to prevent it.§§27, 29

What it protects

  • Liability for aiding requires identifying the main offender and proving knowledge or intent.§27

Where it falls short

  • Officers carry the burden of proving their innocence.
  • Aiding draws the same penalty as the main offense, however small the involvement.

Sexual harms and blackmail

§25

The Act criminalizes content, including AI-generated content, created, obtained or stored for sexual harassment, “revenge porn,” child sexual abuse material, sextortion or blackmail.

Hacking, fraud and other cyber offenses

§§17–24

Other offenses cover illegal access to critical infrastructure and computer systems, phishing, malware and cyber ransom, online gambling, cyber forgery, cyber terrorism and e-transactions without legal authority.

Search, seizure and arrest powers

§§33–35

Officers can seize digital evidence and devices and arrest people, often without a warrant.

Compelled information, secrecy and evidence

§§38, 39, 43

Evidence obtained under the Act may be admitted notwithstanding contrary provisions in the Evidence Act, 1872 or other laws.

When company officers are liable

§29

Company officials are liable unless they show the offense occurred without their knowledge or that they exercised due diligence to prevent it.

What happens to cases under the old laws

§50

The Act repeals the Cyber Security Act, 2023 and sets aside speech-related cases under that law and the Digital Security Act, 2018, while retaining non-speech cases. It does not clarify the status of cases under the Information and Communication Technology Act, 2006.

Analysis

Where the framework falls short.

The Cyber Protection Act 2026 repeats the flaws of its predecessors (Digital Security Act, Cyber Security Act)

What the law provides

  • Both speech offenses are bailable as a matter of right.§§25, 26
  • A magistrate reviews the evidence within 24 hours and can dismiss the complaint and order release.§31(3)–(4)
  • Only a person directly aggrieved, a representative or a law-enforcement officer may file a case.§40
  • Filing a false or malicious complaint is itself an offense.§28

Order reversed

Removal before a court reviews it

§8

The removal power is executive-led: it offers no channel for users to report harmful content and no prior judicial check.

Undefined

Identity information and hatred

§26

The provision addresses use or transmission of identity information for religious disharmony or communal or ethnic hatred. The communication must lead to violence, fear or disorder; these terms and the causal threshold remain unclear.

Undefined

The undefined scope of gambling

§20

“Gambling” is undefined, so online gaming, fantasy sports, esports and promotional contests could be caught.

Bundled

Different harms, one penalty

§25

One provision gathers distinct harms under a single penalty, whatever their severity.

Too wide

Security research is not distinguished

§§17–19

The illegal-access provisions could expose good-faith security researchers to criminal liability. Identifying a vulnerability to improve security is not clearly distinguished from malicious access. This is a risk identified in our analysis, not a report that the researcher was prosecuted.

Too wide

Platforms lose safe-harbor protection

The Act removes, in its entirety, the safe-harbor protection that existed under earlier laws, so platforms can be liable for users’ content.

Burden flipped

Officers carry the burden of proof

§29

Company officers must prove they did not know or tried to prevent the offense, reversing the usual burden of proof.

Who decides

The cyber agency’s independence

§§5, 12

The cyber agency is meant to be an independent statutory authority, yet it sits under a ministerial division and its powers are left to future rules.

8 law-enforcement and intelligence agencies on a national council of about 24 members, working under the prime minister’s direction.

Doubled

One act, two laws

§25

The same conduct, such as “revenge porn” or child sexual abuse material, is punishable under this Act and under the Pornography Control Act, with different penalties.

Recommendation

Amend or replace.

Distinguish between different harms

We recommend splitting the offenses into clearly defined categories: CSAM, non-consensual intimate imagery, deepfakes, sextortion, grooming, cyberstalking, doxxing and more.

Referenced to show different approaches, not as models for adoption.

Keeps conditional liability exemptions for intermediaries while adding due-diligence, notice-and-action, transparency and systemic risk-mitigation duties.
