Laws meant to protect citizens, but susceptible to misuse and rights infringements.

These laws aim to secure cyberspace, regulate digital services and protect the public interest. They frequently do so through overbroad powers, vague definitions, weak safeguards and disproportionate enforcement that put constitutional rights at risk.

Cyber Protection Act

Meant to protect

Its offenses cover content used for sexual harassment, “revenge porn,” child sexual abuse material, sextortion and blackmail.§25

Open to misuse

It also criminalizes online conduct through vague terms such as “disorder” or threats to “communal harmony,” inviting subjective interpretation and overbroad enforcement.

Personal Data Protection Act

Meant to protect

You can correct, update or delete your data, withdraw consent, and object to processing.§§12–14

Open to misuse

Broad exemptions for law enforcement, intelligence, national security and public authorities may let surveillance and data-sharing continue with limited accountability.

Bangladesh Telecommunication Act

Meant to protect

Interception is subject to approval requirements and procedural safeguards against misuse.§97A

Open to misuse

It continues to authorize extensive surveillance, interception and information-control powers with limited independent oversight.

Penal Code

Meant to protect

Its offense of insulting a woman’s “modesty” aims to protect women from harassment and safeguard their dignity.§509

Open to misuse

“Modesty” and “insult” are undefined, and the offense’s nineteenth-century colonial origins leave it ill-suited to contemporary constitutional and social contexts.

Behind the paradox

Where protection falls short

  • Overbroad powers
  • Vague definitions
  • Weak safeguards
  • Selective, ineffective enforcement
  • Weak institutional independence and readiness

Governance logic behind the laws

  • Colonial-era legal architecture
  • Security-first governance approach
  • Restrictions-first legal design

How laws are made

  • State-centric, top-down policymaking
  • Excessive executive rulemaking
  • Without meaningful public participation

Start with your digital life.

Fifteen everyday situations: what the law says, what it protects, and where it falls short.

Speaking and being seen

You post something online.

Posting online is not, by itself, an offense.

What the law says

The cyber law covers sexual harms, blackmail and identity-based hatred. The Penal Code keeps colonial-era speech offenses on sedition, religious feelings and public order. The telecom law penalizes “false or fraudulent” messages.

What it protects

  • Both speech offenses are bailable as a matter of right.§§25, 26
  • Arrest for the identity-based hatred offense requires a warrant.§26

Where it falls short

  • “Hatred,” “fear” and “disorder” are undefined, and the harm must be traced back to the post.
  • Under the Penal Code, a mere attempt or likelihood can be enough, with penalties up to life imprisonment.

Your post is removed or blocked.

Who ordered removal, and when does a tribunal review it?

What the law says

Law-enforcement agencies, the telecom regulator and the cyber agency can each start a removal or block. A tribunal must approve it within three days. The telecom law also lets its regulator act on intermediaries and social media.

What it protects

  • If a tribunal has not approved a removal in time, the content must be restored.§8(4)

Where it falls short

  • The removal can happen before any judge has looked at it.
  • There is no route for users to report harmful content, and no notice, challenge or appeal.

Laws that reach this

  • Cyber Protection Act
  • Telecommunication Act

Someone files a complaint about you.

A complaint, an arrest and a conviction are three different things.

What the law says

Only a person directly affected, someone authorized to act for them, or a law-enforcement officer may file a case under the cyber law. One speech offense allows arrest without a warrant; the other needs one.

What it protects

  • Filing a false or malicious complaint is itself an offense.§28
  • A magistrate reviews the evidence within 24 hours and can dismiss the complaint and order release.§31(3)–(4)
  • Speech cases under the 2023 and 2018 cyber laws are deemed set aside.§50

Where it falls short

  • No evidentiary threshold applies before an investigation, arrest or other coercive step begins.
  • The law does not define a “lawful” or “just” complaint, which weakens the penalty for false ones.

Someone says your post harmed their reputation.

A statement about someone can become a criminal case.

What the law says

Criminal defamation carries up to two years’ imprisonment and/or a fine. The Code sets out ten exceptions, including truth for the public good and comment on the conduct of public servants.

What it protects

  • Criminal defamation carries ten exceptions, including truth for the public good and comment on public servants.§499

Where it falls short

  • Those exceptions do not remove the threat of criminal proceedings.
  • How they are interpreted varies, which leaves people discussing matters of public interest uncertain.

Someone shares an intimate image of you.

Two laws cover this act, with different penalties and bail rules.

What the law says

The cyber law covers content used for sexual harassment, “revenge porn,” child sexual abuse material, sextortion and blackmail. The pornography law also covers this material, with separate penalties. The Penal Code punishes insulting a woman’s “modesty.”

What it protects

  • Threats to publish intimate material are covered, not only publication.§25
  • Penalties are graded, with heavier punishment for child pornography than for adult material.§8

Where it falls short

  • These harms count only if they have a “harmful or intimidating” effect.
  • “Revenge porn” is defined narrowly and requires proving intent to cause harm.

Laws that reach this

  • Cyber Protection Act
  • Pornography Control Act
  • Penal Code

Your data and your devices

An organization holds your data.

You gain rights over your data; others gain grounds to use it without asking.

What the law says

The data-protection law makes consent one basis for using your data. It also allows use without consent for reasons such as a contract, a legal duty or protecting life or health.

What it protects

  • You can correct, update or delete your data, withdraw consent, and object to processing.§§12–14
  • Approved changes and deletions must carry across connected systems.§14

Where it falls short

  • Broad alternative grounds can make consent functionally optional.
  • Deletion needs safeguards where personal data appears in journalism or other public-interest content.

Laws that reach this

  • Data Protection Act

A breach exposes your data.

A breach does not, by itself, have to be reported.

What the law says

An organization must report a breach to the data authority only if it is likely to cause significant damage. For national systems such as the data exchange, the data-management law names security principles but leaves detailed standards to future rules.

What it protects

  • A breach likely to cause significant damage must be reported to the data authority.§20
  • Zero-trust architecture, privacy-preserving principles, auditability and minimization are named in the Act.§30

Where it falls short

  • An unclear “significant” threshold leaves room for underreporting.
  • Incident response, encryption standards and vulnerability disclosure are left undefined or to future regulations.

Laws that reach this

  • Data Protection Act

Your child goes online.

Your consent is needed for your child’s data, but the law does not say how it is checked.

What the law says

Using a child’s data needs a parent’s or guardian’s consent, and tracking, profiling and targeted ads aimed at children are prohibited. The cyber law covers child sexual abuse material, and the pornography law punishes child pornography more heavily than adult material.

What it protects

  • Behavioral tracking, profiling and targeted ads directed at children are prohibited.§9
  • Penalties are graded, with heavier punishment for child pornography than for adult material.§8

Where it falls short

  • The law does not say how parental consent is given or verified, or how age is assessed.
  • Parental consent can stay valid until 18, with no step for the young adult’s own consent.

Your records are linked across public services.

Six foundational records flow into one national exchange.

What the law says

National ID, passport, tax, address, birth registration and driving-license databases must connect to a national data exchange, with storage in national data centers where deemed expedient. The government may classify “nationally important / sensitive data” and restrict its exchange.

What it protects

  • The Act declares the data authority independent.§27
  • Zero-trust architecture, privacy-preserving principles, auditability and minimization are named in the Act.§30

Where it falls short

  • Linking identity records makes detailed profiles easier to build, with limited safeguards against reuse.
  • The “nationally important” category has no objective criteria, independent review or route to challenge.

  • Data Management Act

Your calls and messages are monitored.

Monitoring can begin first; a council reviews it afterwards.

What the law says

The telecom law sets up a central interception platform under the Ministry of Home Affairs, open to multiple agencies, and intercepted material can be used as evidence. The data-protection law exempts the state from consent on grounds such as national security and public order.

What it protects

  • Interception is subject to approval requirements and procedural safeguards against misuse.§97A
  • Where an exemption applies, the other data-protection duties still do.§24(2)
  • The data authority may review misuse of an exemption.§24(5)

Where it falls short

  • The enacted law dropped the draft’s requirement of prior authorization by an independent council.
  • An urgency exception allows interception without prior approval.

Your phone or laptop is searched.

A search for one offense can expose data unrelated to it.

What the law says

Cyber-law investigators can search, seize devices and data, and arrest, often without a warrant. The pornography law gives similar search and seizure powers. Telecom inspectors can access data, arrest without a warrant and seize equipment.

What it protects

  • A magistrate reviews the evidence within 24 hours and can dismiss the complaint and order release.§31(3)–(4)

Where it falls short

  • None of these laws makes probable cause a precondition, and none sets rules for handling seized data.
  • Seized devices and data can be presented openly in court, exposing private conduct.

Laws that reach this

  • Cyber Protection Act

Money, platforms and harm

You use a “free” service.

Paying with data and attention falls outside consumer law’s idea of a buyer.

What the law says

Consumer law protects people who buy goods or services for payment, and its service definition excludes services with no direct payment. Competition law prohibits anti-competitive agreements and abuse of a dominant position, judged largely by price and geography.

What it protects

  • Defective goods, misleading ads, pricing irregularities and failed services are prohibited practices.§§44, 45
  • Its abuse-of-dominance terms are, in principle, broad enough to reach self-preferencing, tying and bundling.§16(2)

Where it falls short

  • Data and attention are not recognized as payment, so “free” services sit outside consumer protection.
  • A price-centric market definition misses network effects and data power.

Laws that reach this

  • Consumer Rights Act
  • Competition Act

You buy something online.

A seller who takes your money and fails to deliver breaks the law, but an offshore seller may be out of reach.

What the law says

Defective goods, misleading advertisements, pricing irregularities and failing to provide a paid-for service are anti-consumer practices, and the consumer directorate’s Director General can enforce the Act.

What it protects

  • Defective goods, misleading ads, pricing irregularities and failed services are prohibited practices.§§44, 45
  • The consumer directorate’s Director General holds an enforcement mandate.§21

Where it falls short

  • The Act does not define its territorial reach, so an offshore seller may fall outside it.
  • Confusing consent buttons, hard-to-cancel subscriptions and other manipulative designs are not adequately addressed.

Laws that reach this

  • Consumer Rights Act

You run an online shop, page or platform.

You can be held liable for helping an offense committed through your service.

What the law says

Under the cyber law, a platform can be held liable for aiding an offense, and its owners and managers must then prove they did not know or tried to prevent it. The telecom law now reaches online intermediaries, and the Penal Code makes offering prizes or rewards an offense.

What it protects

  • Liability for aiding requires identifying the main offender and proving knowledge or intent.§27

Where it falls short

  • It is unclear how the telecom law’s licensing requirement applies to digital platforms.
  • The ban on offering prizes or rewards could reach discount codes, cashback and loyalty programs.

Laws that reach this

  • Cyber Protection Act
  • Telecommunication Act
  • Penal Code

Someone hacks your account or scams you online.

Hacking and online fraud are crimes, but one penalty covers very different acts.

What the law says

The cyber law criminalizes illegal access to devices, systems and networks, phishing, malware and ransomware, cyber forgery and e-transactions without legal authority.

What it protects

  • Illegal access to a device or network, phishing, malware and ransomware are offenses.§§18, 19

Where it falls short

  • Collecting data without authorization is treated as gravely as spreading malware or sending phishing or ransomware mail.
  • The same broad offenses can catch ethical hacking, penetration testing and security research, which weakens security overall.

Laws that reach this

  • Cyber Protection Act
